Quality and bitrate

Bitrate is how many bits each second of sound gets, and it's the biggest lever you have. More bits sound better, until you can't hear the difference.

The basics

10

Make this point first: for anyone using a codec, rather than writing one, bitrate (or the quality slider that stands in for it) is the most important lever you have. Once the codec is picked, almost every other setting follows from it: it decides how good the sound is and how big the file is, and with most perceptual codecs it moves the decode cost too. It is the setting you will touch most, per sound or per sound bank, so it comes before compression ratios. Bitrate is how many bits each second of sound gets, and for perceptual codecs it is the main quality control. More bits sound better, with diminishing returns: past a point listeners cannot tell it from the original, which is called transparent, and extra bits only cost size. The left column gives real numbers. These are the Opus sweet spots from RFC 7587, the standard for carrying Opus over the network, at 20 ms frames and full bandwidth: 28 to 40 kbps for speech, 48 to 64 for mono music, 64 to 128 for stereo music. Xiph's own recommended-settings page says Opus at 128 kbps VBR is pretty much transparent. For scale, ADPCM has no bitrate setting: 4 bits a sample at 48 kHz stereo is always 384 kbps, three to six times what Opus needs for music, which is the size side of ADPCM's cheap decode. PCM is 1,536. Constant or variable: constant bitrate (CBR) gives every second the same number of bits, so the size is predictable, which is why network streaming and voice chat often use it. Variable bitrate (VBR) spends more bits on busy, complex passages and fewer on quiet or simple ones, so it sounds better at the same average size, but the final size depends on the content. Vorbis in Wwise and many other tools expose a quality value instead of a bitrate; that is VBR under the hood. What too low sounds like, so people know what to listen for: pre-echo, where a sharp transient smears into the silence before it; swirly or warbling high frequencies; and a dull top end, because encoders cut the highest frequencies first at low rates. These numbers are for Opus; other codecs need different bitrates for the same quality, so always listen on target speakers and headphones. Sources: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7587 (section 3.1.1) and https://wiki.xiph.org/Opus_Recommended_Settings
