The basics

10

Every codec is lossless or lossy, and lossy codecs come in a few families. Game assets mostly use two of them, predictive and perceptual, which follow rules people designed. Neural codecs are the clean break: they learn their own encoder and decoder from examples, and the decoder generates sound rather than reconstructing it. They are mostly research today and come back in the frontier section. If someone asks about speech codecs like SILK, Speex or AMR: they mix the predictive and perceptual ideas, fitting a model of the voice to each short chunk, so they are not on the table as a separate family; SILK is the speech mode inside Opus. Lossless, like FLAC, predicts each sample and stores the error exactly, so everything comes back. Predictive lossy codecs, like ADPCM, do the same prediction but store only a rough version of the error: a little noise, and almost no CPU to decode. Perceptual codecs, like Vorbis, Opus and AAC, move to the frequency domain and spend bits according to what the ear can hear, so they get much smaller but cost the most CPU. The slide only names the families and where they are used, so say how each works out loud. PCM is not on this slide because it is not really a codec: it is the uncompressed baseline everything else is measured against, even though engines list it in the same dropdown. Lossless like FLAC barely shows up in shipped games because it only gets about half the size of PCM. The ADPCM family is everywhere: every platform and middleware has its own flavour. The perceptual row is most of what we will talk about in the toolbox section, including the platform ones, XMA on Xbox and ATRAC9 on PlayStation.
