The basics

14

~1.5 min. Perceptual codecs, which is most of what games ship (Vorbis, Opus, AAC, XMA, ATRAC9, Bink Audio), work in frequency, a block at a time. Walk the pipeline: cut the audio into short blocks, typically around 20 ms; transform each block from a waveform into frequencies, usually with a transform called the MDCT; then the lossy step. Walk the diagram: a loud sound hides quieter sounds close to it in frequency, and for a moment in time too. That is masking, and it is how hearing works, not a flaw in the codec. A model of hearing works out where that masking curve sits, and anything under it, like the dashed bar, gets few bits or none. A quiet sound outside the curve, on the right, is still audible, so it keeps its bits. Finally the result is packed tightly with entropy coding. Because how much to throw away is a choice, the size is a setting, the bitrate on the next slide, and 10:1 or more is normal. The cost is on the decode side: the transform has to run backwards for every block of every playing voice, which is why perceptual codecs cost more CPU than ADPCM. When pushed too far, what goes is what the model thought you would not miss: smeared transients, swirly highs, a dull top end.
