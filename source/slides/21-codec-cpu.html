The basics

21

Start with the diagram. PCM that already matches the mix format (sample rate, channel layout, sample format) goes straight from memory or disk to the mixer: there is nothing to do. A compressed sound has to be decoded back to PCM first, and that happens for every voice that is playing, every time it plays. If PCM doesn't match the mix format it still needs converting, for example resampling, but it never needs decoding. Hardware decoders on consoles and phones take the decode step off the CPU. Decode cost is paid per playing voice, every time a sound plays, so what matters is the cost at your busiest moment, not for one sound in isolation. Audiokinetic measured how many streams each codec can decode at once in Wwise on a 2018 Core i7 PC: about 9,500 for ADPCM, 2,300 to 5,700 for Vorbis and 500 to 1,300 for Opus. The spread within Vorbis and Opus comes from the quality setting; higher quality costs more. Read it as a ratio, not a budget: no game spends its whole CPU on audio decode, but ADPCM is several times cheaper than Vorbis and Vorbis several times cheaper than Opus. That is the other side of the compression-ratio slide: the smallest files cost the most to play. This is why ADPCM survives for short, busy sounds, and why consoles and phones put decoders in hardware (XMA on Xbox, AAC on phones): it takes this cost off the CPU entirely. Bink Audio and QOA both aim for perceptual-codec size at close to ADPCM's cost, which comes up later. Numbers vary by platform; Audiokinetic's article has figures for each, so check yours.
