~2 min. These two families are what almost every runtime game asset uses, so it is worth one slide on how each works. The red box in each row is where the loss happens. Predictive, ADPCM: it works in time, one sample at a time. It guesses the next sample from the last few, works out how wrong the guess was, and stores that error rounded to 4 bits. The adaptive part is the step size: it grows when the signal is busy and shrinks when it is quiet, so 4 bits stretch further. The decoder makes the same guesses and adds the stored error back, which is only a few additions per sample, and that is why ADPCM is nearly free to play. What it loses is fine detail, which you hear as a steady low-level hiss, most on bright sounds. Every sample costs the same 4 bits, so the size is fixed at about 4:1. Perceptual, Vorbis, Opus, AAC and the rest: it works in frequency, a block at a time. It cuts the audio into short blocks, typically around 20 ms, and transforms each into frequencies, usually with a transform called the MDCT. Then comes the lossy step: a model of hearing works out which frequencies are masked, hidden by louder sounds close to them in frequency or time, and spends fewer bits or none there. The rest is packed tightly. Because how much to throw away is a choice, the size is a setting, the bitrate from the next slide, and 10:1 or more is normal. The decoder has to run the transform back for every block of every voice, which is why perceptual codecs cost more CPU. The takeaway: predictive is cheap and fixed-size, perceptual is small and tunable but costs CPU. Everything in the next few slides, bitrate, ratio and CPU, follows from these two pipelines.
