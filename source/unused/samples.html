Consoles started with ADPCM in tiny sound RAM, then moved perceptual codecs into hardware decoders. PCs had the memory and CPU to decode in software.

The past

25

From kilobytes of sound RAM to hardware decoders. Early consoles made sound by synthesis; once they played back recorded samples, the samples had to fit in almost no memory. The SNES had 64 KB of audio RAM, so it stored samples as BRR, an ADPCM-style format at about 3.5:1. The PlayStation decoded 4-bit ADPCM on 24 voices from 512 KB, and streamed longer music and speech from the CD as XA-ADPCM. The PS2 kept the idea with 2 MB and 48 voices. Then the model flips: memory becomes shared system RAM measured in gigabytes, and the console's job is to decode a perceptual codec in hardware so the CPU doesn't have to. Xbox 360 decoded XMA in hardware, up to 320 voices; Xbox One's SHAPE audio block raised that to 512 XMA voices; Xbox Series X|S moved its hardware decoder to Opus. PlayStation 4 used ATRAC9, Sony's codec optimized for games. Meanwhile on PC: the original Sound Blaster (1989) played 8-bit mono samples and could decompress ADPCM on the card; from the mid-1990s many games streamed music as CD audio tracks; the Sound Blaster Live! (1998) had 64 hardware voices and kept samples in system RAM; by the mid-2000s, games decoded MP3 and Vorbis on the CPU, and by the 2010s middleware like Wwise and FMOD handled it, with Opus (2012) and later Bink Audio joining Vorbis. PCs always had the memory and CPU to decode in software.
